# Crypto Trading Risk Management

## What is risk management?

Risk management is the process of identifying, measuring, and controlling potential losses.

In crypto trading, risk management is particularly important because cryptocurrency markets can experience substantial volatility and rapid price movements.

Risk management cannot eliminate losses, but it can help limit the impact of individual trades.

## Capital preservation

Capital preservation refers to protecting trading capital from large or unnecessary losses.

A trader who experiences a very large loss may require a disproportionately larger gain to recover.

For example, a 50% loss requires a 100% gain on the remaining capital to return to the original amount.

## Risk per trade

Risk per trade refers to the amount of capital a trader is willing to lose if a trade reaches its predefined invalidation or stop-loss condition.

Some trading frameworks use a small percentage of account equity as a maximum risk per trade.

The appropriate percentage depends on the individual's circumstances, strategy, and risk tolerance.

There is no universally correct percentage.

## Position sizing

Position sizing determines how much of an asset is purchased or sold for a trade.

A simplified position-sizing framework can be expressed as:

Position Size = Amount Willing to Risk / Risk Per Unit

For example, if a trader is willing to risk $100 and the planned loss per unit is $5, the theoretical position size is 20 units.

Actual trading outcomes can differ because of slippage, fees, gaps, and execution conditions.

## Stop-loss and risk management

A stop-loss can be used to define a point at which a trade thesis is considered invalid or a maximum planned loss is reached.

A stop-loss should be determined as part of the trading strategy rather than placed arbitrarily.

A stop-loss does not guarantee that the actual loss will equal the planned amount.

## Take-profit and risk management

Take-profit levels can define predefined exit points for profitable trades.

Using predefined exits can reduce emotional decision-making.

However, a take-profit does not guarantee that the order will execute at the specified price.

## Risk-reward ratio

Risk-reward ratio compares potential loss with potential gain.

For example, a trade with a potential loss of $100 and a potential gain of $300 has a 1:3 risk-reward relationship.

A high potential reward relative to risk does not guarantee that the trade will succeed.

## Expected value

Expected value is a statistical concept used to estimate the average outcome of a strategy over many trades.

A simplified model is:

Expected Value = (Probability of Win × Average Win) - (Probability of Loss × Average Loss)

A strategy can have positive expected value while still experiencing losing trades and losing streaks.

Expected value should be evaluated over a sufficiently large sample.

## Win rate

Win rate is the percentage of trades that result in a profit according to a defined measurement.

A high win rate does not necessarily mean a strategy is profitable.

For example, a strategy can have many small winning trades and occasional very large losses.

Win rate should be evaluated together with average win, average loss, fees, and drawdown.

## Drawdown

Drawdown measures the decline in portfolio or strategy value from a previous peak.

For example, if an account decreases from $10,000 to $8,000, the drawdown from that peak is 20%.

Large drawdowns can be difficult to recover from.

## Drawdown recovery

The gain required to recover from a loss is larger than the percentage loss.

Examples:

- 10% loss requires approximately 11.1% gain to recover.
- 20% loss requires 25% gain.
- 30% loss requires approximately 42.9% gain.
- 50% loss requires 100% gain.
- 75% loss requires 300% gain.

This demonstrates why controlling downside risk is important.

## Leverage risk

Leverage increases the size of a position relative to the trader's margin.

While leverage can increase potential returns on margin, it also increases potential losses.

High leverage can cause relatively small price movements to produce large changes in account equity.

## Liquidation risk

Leveraged positions can be liquidated when available margin falls below required levels.

Liquidation can occur rapidly during volatile market conditions.

A trader should understand the liquidation mechanism, maintenance margin, fees, and margin requirements of the specific platform before using leverage.

## Cross margin

Under cross margin systems, available account collateral may be shared across positions according to the platform's rules.

This can increase the amount of collateral supporting a position but can also expose more account capital to losses.

Exact behavior varies by exchange.

## Isolated margin

Under isolated margin systems, a specific amount of margin is assigned to a position.

The maximum loss related to that position's margin may therefore be more contained than under cross margin, subject to platform rules.

Traders should understand the exact implementation before relying on this distinction.

## Diversification

Diversification involves spreading exposure across different assets or strategies.

Diversification can reduce concentration risk.

However, assets that appear different can become highly correlated during periods of market stress.

Diversification does not guarantee protection against losses.

## Correlation

Correlation describes the degree to which two assets' returns move together.

Highly correlated assets can provide less diversification benefit than expected.

Crypto assets can experience periods in which many assets move in the same direction.

## Concentration risk

Concentration risk occurs when a large proportion of capital is exposed to a single asset, sector, protocol, or strategy.

A concentrated position can produce large gains but also large losses.

## Liquidity risk

Liquidity risk is the risk that an asset cannot be bought or sold at the expected price because there is insufficient market depth.

Low liquidity can lead to:

- Wider spreads
- Greater slippage
- Difficulty exiting positions
- Larger price impact

Liquidity should be considered before entering a position.

## Slippage risk

Slippage occurs when the actual execution price differs from the expected price.

Slippage can become significant during volatile markets or when trading large orders in low-liquidity markets.

## Gap and fast-market risk

Crypto markets can move rapidly.

During sudden price movements, an order may execute at a substantially different price from the intended level.

Risk-management calculations should account for the possibility that stop-loss execution is imperfect.

## Exchange risk

Using a cryptocurrency exchange introduces counterparty and operational risks.

Potential risks include:

- Exchange outages
- Security breaches
- Withdrawal restrictions
- Operational failures
- Regulatory changes
- Counterparty failure

Users should understand the risks and protections associated with the platform they use.

## Custody risk

Holding crypto assets requires consideration of custody.

Custodial platforms manage keys on behalf of users.

Non-custodial users manage their own keys.

Both approaches have different security and operational risks.

## Private-key security

Private keys and recovery phrases should be treated as highly sensitive security information.

They should not be shared with other people or entered into unknown websites.

Phishing attacks may attempt to trick users into revealing recovery information.

## Phishing

Phishing is a form of social engineering in which attackers attempt to obtain sensitive information by pretending to be a trusted person, platform, or service.

Users should verify URLs, wallet prompts, transaction details, and communications before approving transactions.

## Smart-contract risk

Interacting with decentralized applications introduces smart-contract risk.

A smart contract can contain coding errors, vulnerabilities, economic weaknesses, or unexpected behavior.

Users should not assume that a smart contract is safe merely because it is widely used.

## Emotional risk

Trading decisions can be affected by emotions such as:

- Fear
- Greed
- FOMO
- Revenge trading
- Overconfidence
- Panic selling

A predefined trading plan can help reduce impulsive decisions.

## FOMO

FOMO means "fear of missing out."

In trading, FOMO can cause a person to enter a position because an asset has already moved significantly.

Entering a trade without a defined risk plan can increase the probability of poor decisions.

## Revenge trading

Revenge trading occurs when a trader attempts to recover losses quickly by taking additional or larger positions.

This can lead to increasing risk after losses and potentially larger drawdowns.

## Trading journal

A trading journal records information about trades and decisions.

Useful fields can include:

- Date
- Asset
- Entry
- Exit
- Position size
- Stop-loss
- Take-profit
- Strategy
- Reason for entry
- Result
- Fees
- Emotional state
- Lessons learned

A journal can help identify recurring mistakes and evaluate strategy performance.

## Risk limits

A trading plan can define limits such as:

- Maximum risk per trade
- Maximum daily loss
- Maximum weekly loss
- Maximum leverage
- Maximum portfolio concentration
- Maximum number of simultaneous positions

Risk limits should be established before trading rather than after a loss occurs.

## Risk of ruin

Risk of ruin refers to the possibility that repeated losses reduce trading capital to a level where continuing the strategy becomes impractical.

High leverage, excessive position sizes, and large risk per trade can substantially increase this risk.

## Stop-loss limitations

A stop-loss is not a guarantee against losses.

During extreme volatility, the execution price may be worse than the stop level.

A stop-loss also does not protect against every type of risk, such as exchange downtime or technical failures.

## Stablecoin risks

Stablecoins are designed to maintain relatively stable values but can experience deviations from their intended reference value.

Risks can include:

- Reserve risk
- Counterparty risk
- Liquidity risk
- Depegging
- Regulatory risk
- Smart-contract risk

Stablecoins should not automatically be treated as equivalent to cash.

## Risk management checklist

Before entering a trade, a trader can consider:

1. What is the trading thesis?
2. Where is the entry?
3. Where is the trade invalidated?
4. What is the planned maximum loss?
5. What is the position size?
6. What fees and slippage could occur?
7. Is leverage being used?
8. What is the liquidation risk?
9. Is the position too concentrated?
10. Does the trade fit the overall risk plan?

## Important principle

Risk management should focus on controlling downside rather than attempting to guarantee profits.

No position-sizing method, stop-loss strategy, indicator, or risk-reward ratio can guarantee a profitable trade.
