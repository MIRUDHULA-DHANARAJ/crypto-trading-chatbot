# Crypto Trading Basics

## What is trading?

Trading involves buying and selling an asset with the objective of managing or potentially profiting from price movements.

Crypto trading can occur across different market types, including spot markets and derivative markets.

Trading outcomes are uncertain and losses are possible.

## Spot trading

Spot trading involves buying or selling an asset for settlement according to the rules of the relevant market.

When a trader buys BTC through a spot market, the trader generally receives the underlying asset in the trading account or wallet.

Spot trading does not inherently involve leverage.

## Futures trading

Futures are derivative contracts whose value is linked to an underlying asset.

Crypto futures can allow traders to take long or short positions without directly owning the underlying asset.

Futures may use leverage and margin.

The exact contract specifications vary between exchanges.

## Long position

A long position generally expresses an expectation that the asset price will increase.

For example, if a trader buys an asset at $100 and later sells it at $120, the gross price difference is $20 per unit before fees and other costs.

If the asset price falls instead, the long position can lose value.

## Short position

A short position generally expresses an expectation that the asset price will decrease.

In derivative markets, traders can often open short positions without borrowing and selling the underlying asset directly.

If the price decreases, the position may gain value.

If the price increases, the position can lose value.

## Market order

A market order is intended to execute immediately at the best available prices in the market.

The exact execution price is not guaranteed.

Market orders can experience slippage, particularly when liquidity is low or price movements are rapid.

## Limit order

A limit order specifies a price condition for execution.

A buy limit order generally specifies the maximum price the trader is willing to pay.

A sell limit order generally specifies the minimum price the trader is willing to accept.

A limit order may remain unfilled if the market does not reach the specified price.

## Stop order

A stop order uses a trigger price to activate an order.

The exact behavior depends on the exchange and order type.

Stop orders are often used as part of risk-management or trade-entry strategies.

## Stop-loss

A stop-loss is a predefined mechanism or level intended to limit losses on a position.

A stop-loss does not guarantee execution at the exact selected price.

During rapid price movements, gaps, or low liquidity, the actual execution price may differ from the intended level.

## Take-profit

A take-profit level is a predefined price at which a trader intends to exit a position to realize a potential profit.

Take-profit orders can help traders follow predefined trade plans rather than making decisions entirely during market movement.

They do not guarantee a profitable outcome.

## Bid and ask

The bid is the highest price that buyers are currently willing to pay.

The ask is the lowest price that sellers are currently willing to accept.

The difference between the best ask and best bid is called the bid-ask spread.

## Spread

The bid-ask spread represents the difference between the best available buying and selling prices.

A narrower spread generally indicates lower immediate trading friction.

A wider spread can increase the cost of entering and exiting positions.

## Slippage

Slippage occurs when the execution price differs from the expected price.

It can occur because of rapid market movement, insufficient liquidity, or large order size.

Market orders can be particularly susceptible to slippage in thin markets.

## Liquidity

Liquidity refers to how easily an asset can be bought or sold without substantially affecting its price.

Highly liquid markets generally have greater trading activity and more available orders.

Lower liquidity can increase spread and slippage.

## Trading volume

Trading volume measures the amount of an asset traded during a specified period.

Volume is often analyzed alongside price to understand market activity.

High volume does not automatically mean that price will rise or fall.

## Leverage

Leverage allows a trader to control a position larger than the trader's initial margin.

For example, with 5x leverage, a trader may control a position approximately five times the amount of margin, subject to the exchange's rules.

Leverage magnifies both potential gains and potential losses.

## Margin

Margin is collateral used to support a leveraged trading position.

Different exchanges and derivative products use different margin systems.

If the value of a position declines sufficiently, additional margin may be required or the position may be liquidated.

## Liquidation

Liquidation occurs when an exchange or trading platform forcibly closes a leveraged position because margin requirements are no longer satisfied.

Liquidation can occur before a trader loses the entire nominal position value.

Exact liquidation calculations depend on factors such as leverage, entry price, maintenance margin, fees, and exchange-specific rules.

## Funding rate

In some perpetual futures markets, funding payments are exchanged between long and short traders.

The funding rate helps keep the perpetual contract price aligned with the underlying market.

A positive funding rate can mean that long-position holders pay short-position holders, while a negative rate can reverse the direction.

The exact mechanism varies by platform.

## Perpetual futures

Perpetual futures are derivative contracts that do not have a traditional expiration date.

They typically use funding mechanisms to help keep the contract price close to the underlying asset's price.

Perpetual futures can be highly leveraged and carry significant liquidation risk.

## Trading timeframe

A trading timeframe represents the period covered by each chart candle.

Common timeframes include:

- 1 minute
- 5 minutes
- 15 minutes
- 1 hour
- 4 hours
- 1 day
- 1 week

Different timeframes can show different market structures.

## Trend

A trend describes the general direction of market movement.

An uptrend generally contains a sequence of higher highs and higher lows.

A downtrend generally contains lower highs and lower lows.

A market can also move sideways without a clear directional trend.

## Breakout

A breakout occurs when price moves beyond a previously observed support or resistance area.

Breakouts can be genuine or false.

Traders may examine volume, momentum, and subsequent price behavior when evaluating a breakout.

A breakout does not guarantee continuation.

## Pullback

A pullback is a temporary movement against the prevailing trend.

For example, a decline during a broader upward movement may be described as a pullback.

A pullback should not automatically be interpreted as a trend reversal.

## Support

Support is a price area where buying interest has historically helped slow or stop a decline.

Support is not a guaranteed floor.

If selling pressure becomes strong enough, price can move below a support area.

## Resistance

Resistance is a price area where selling interest has historically helped slow or stop an increase.

Resistance is not guaranteed to hold.

Price can move above resistance when buying pressure becomes sufficiently strong.

## Technical indicators

Technical indicators are mathematical calculations derived from market data such as price and volume.

Common indicators include:

- RSI
- Moving averages
- MACD
- Bollinger Bands
- VWAP

Indicators are analytical tools rather than guaranteed prediction mechanisms.

## Trading strategy

A trading strategy is a defined set of rules used to make trading decisions.

A strategy may specify:

- Entry conditions
- Exit conditions
- Stop-loss conditions
- Position size
- Risk limits
- Trading timeframe
- Market conditions

A strategy should be evaluated using appropriate historical and live-market testing methods.

## Backtesting

Backtesting involves evaluating a trading strategy against historical market data.

Backtesting can help identify how a strategy would have performed under specified historical assumptions.

Backtesting has limitations, including:

- Historical data may not represent future conditions.
- Fees may be underestimated.
- Slippage may be ignored.
- Execution assumptions may be unrealistic.
- Overfitting can produce misleading results.

## Paper trading

Paper trading simulates trading without using real capital.

It can help users practice execution and evaluate a strategy.

Paper trading does not perfectly reproduce the psychological and execution conditions of real trading.

## Trading psychology

Trading decisions can be affected by emotions such as fear, greed, overconfidence, and loss aversion.

A predefined trading plan and risk limits can help reduce impulsive decisions.

Emotional discipline does not eliminate financial risk.

## Trading fees

Trading platforms can charge different fees, including maker fees, taker fees, withdrawal fees, and funding-related costs.

Fees can materially affect trading performance, especially for high-frequency strategies.

Traders should review the current fee schedule of the platform they use.

## Important considerations

No trading strategy guarantees profits.

Technical indicators and chart patterns should not be treated as certain prediction tools.

A trader can lose money even when following a well-defined strategy.
