# Ethereum Basics

## What is Ethereum?

Ethereum is a decentralized blockchain platform designed to support programmable smart contracts and decentralized applications.

Ethereum has a native digital asset called Ether, commonly represented by the ticker symbol ETH.

While Bitcoin was primarily designed around decentralized digital money and value transfer, Ethereum was designed as a programmable blockchain capable of executing smart-contract logic.

## What is Ether?

Ether, or ETH, is the native asset of the Ethereum network.

ETH is used for purposes including paying transaction fees and interacting with applications deployed on Ethereum.

ETH can also be traded on cryptocurrency markets.

## Ethereum blockchain

The Ethereum blockchain is a distributed ledger maintained by a network of participants.

Transactions and smart-contract interactions are recorded on the blockchain.

Ethereum uses a proof-of-stake consensus mechanism.

## Proof of stake

Ethereum uses proof of stake to help secure and validate its blockchain.

Validators participate by depositing ETH into the protocol according to Ethereum's staking rules.

Validators are responsible for proposing and attesting to blocks.

The proof-of-stake system differs from Bitcoin's proof-of-work approach.

## Smart contracts

A smart contract is a program deployed on a blockchain.

Smart contracts contain predefined logic that can execute when their conditions are satisfied.

Because smart contracts can directly control blockchain-based assets and operations, vulnerabilities in smart-contract code can result in significant losses.

## Decentralized applications

Decentralized applications, often called dApps, are applications that interact with blockchain-based smart contracts.

Examples of blockchain application categories include decentralized exchanges, lending protocols, NFT platforms, and other financial or non-financial applications.

## Gas fees

Ethereum transactions and smart-contract operations require computational resources.

Users generally pay transaction fees known as gas fees.

The total fee depends on factors including the amount of computational work required and network conditions.

Gas fees can change significantly depending on demand.

## Gas price

Gas price represents the amount a user is willing to pay for computational work on Ethereum.

Gas usage and gas price together contribute to the transaction fee.

A transaction requiring more computational work consumes more gas than a simple transaction.

## Ethereum addresses

Ethereum addresses identify accounts or smart contracts on the Ethereum network.

An Ethereum address is commonly represented as a hexadecimal string beginning with `0x`.

Users should carefully verify addresses before sending assets.

Sending assets to an incorrect or incompatible address may result in permanent loss.

## Ethereum wallets

Ethereum wallets manage the cryptographic keys used to interact with Ethereum accounts and applications.

Wallets can be custodial or non-custodial.

Non-custodial wallets require users to securely manage their private keys or recovery phrases.

## Ethereum tokens

Ethereum supports tokens implemented through smart contracts.

One widely used token standard is ERC-20, which defines common functionality for fungible tokens.

Other standards exist for different asset types and use cases.

A token created on Ethereum is distinct from ETH, which is the native asset of the Ethereum network.

## Stablecoins

Stablecoins are crypto assets designed to maintain a relatively stable value relative to another asset or reference, commonly a fiat currency such as the US dollar.

Different stablecoins use different mechanisms to maintain their intended value.

A stablecoin's design, reserves, collateral, governance, and redemption mechanisms affect its risks.

A stablecoin should not automatically be assumed to be risk-free.

## Decentralized exchanges

A decentralized exchange, or DEX, allows users to trade assets through blockchain-based protocols and smart contracts.

Many DEXs use automated market maker mechanisms rather than a traditional centralized order book.

Users interact directly with smart contracts through a wallet.

DEX transactions can involve network fees, price impact, slippage, and smart-contract risks.

## Automated market makers

An automated market maker, or AMM, is a mechanism used by some decentralized exchanges to facilitate trading.

Instead of matching buyers and sellers through a conventional order book, AMMs commonly use liquidity pools and mathematical pricing mechanisms.

Liquidity providers deposit assets into pools and may receive fees or other incentives according to the protocol.

## Liquidity pools

A liquidity pool is a smart-contract-controlled pool containing assets that can be used by a decentralized trading protocol.

Liquidity providers contribute assets to pools.

Providing liquidity can expose users to risks including impermanent loss, smart-contract vulnerabilities, and changes in asset prices.

## Layer 2 networks

Layer 2 networks are systems designed to process transactions or computation while using Ethereum as a settlement or security layer.

They can help increase transaction throughput and potentially reduce transaction costs compared with executing every operation directly on Ethereum mainnet.

Different Layer 2 systems use different technical architectures.

## Ethereum and trading

ETH can be traded in spot markets and derivative markets.

Trading ETH involves market risks including volatility, liquidity risk, execution risk, and potential losses.

Derivative products can introduce additional risks such as leverage and liquidation.

## Ethereum risks

Important risks associated with Ethereum and Ethereum-based applications include:

- Market volatility
- Smart-contract vulnerabilities
- Private-key loss
- Phishing and wallet attacks
- Network congestion
- Transaction errors
- Liquidity risk
- Protocol-specific risks

Ethereum's technology does not eliminate financial or operational risk.

## Important considerations

Ethereum applications and crypto assets can involve significant technical and financial risks.

Smart-contract functionality does not guarantee safety.

Historical ETH performance and market patterns cannot guarantee future results.
